PUBLIC 1 / UNDERSTAND THE SYSTEM

How Does Council Tax Work?

How bills are set, why a tax based on 1991 values has drifted from today’s housing market, and what this means for effective tax rates.

1 · Band assignments2 · Charging proportions3 · Effective tax rates4 · Lower-value homes

THE BILL

Your band and your council’s charge

In England, Council Tax places homes in eight bands based on their estimated value on 1 April 1991. The council sets a Band D charge each year; the other bands pay fixed proportions of that charge.

A higher band usually means a higher bill in the same area. Councils set different charges, so the same band can have different bills in different places.

THE EFFECTIVE TAX RATE

The bill as a share of property value

The effective tax rate divides the standard annual bill by the property’s value. A £1,500 bill on a £150,000 home is 1%; the same bill on a £500,000 home is 0.3%.

These are illustrative examples. The atlas uses observed sale prices to estimate local rates for properties in the same band.

WHAT IS WRONG WITH THE CURRENT SYSTEM?

A frozen tax base in a changing housing market

Two things can drift: which band a home belongs to, and how much each band is charged relative to the others.

Homes and places have changed in value at different rates. Old bands can group together homes with very different current prices. At the same time, the fixed charging proportions cover a much narrower range than today’s property values.

The charts below separate those two problems. Updating band assignments tackles the first; updating charging proportions tackles the second.

02 / THE CHARGING PROPORTIONS

Do the charging proportions reflect today’s price gaps?

Band D is the reference. The charging proportions are fixed, while property prices change. Compare each band’s median price with the same-year Band D median, then compare that price ratio with its statutory charge ratio.

For example, if a band’s median home is worth three times the Band D median but pays only twice the Band D charge, it pays a smaller share of property value.

04 / THE RESULT: REGRESSIVITY

Lower-value homes face higher tax rates.

A higher bill can still be a lower tax rate. Lower-value homes tend to pay a larger share of their value in Council Tax.

A Band H home pays three times the Band A charge in the same council. But the difference in current property values can be much greater than three times. The figures below show the resulting regressivity relative to property value.

England’s Suits index

Negative means regressive relative to property value; zero means proportional. This is not an income-based measure.
